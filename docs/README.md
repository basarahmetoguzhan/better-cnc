# better-cnc — documentation index

Retrofit of a 3-axis industrial marble CNC from a Windows controller to
Raspberry Pi + LinuxCNC, driving a **Zhulong V2.0** Ethernet motion board
(Mesa 7i96 derivative — Spartan-6 XC6SLX9, Micrel KSZ8851 PHY, `hm2_eth` driver).

**Start here if you have been away from this project.** Read this file, then
[04](04-zhulong-board-hardware.md) for what our board actually is, then
[02](02-board-bringup.md) for what to do next.

### The blockers, in one line each

From [OPEN-QUESTIONS.md](OPEN-QUESTIONS.md) — 🔴 blocks the next phase, 🟡 blocks wiring or commissioning:

| | Question | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| 🔴 **B1** | How many motors on Y? | Opening the cabinet and counting |
| 🔴 **B2** | Can the encoder pins be used as GPIO? | `readhmid` + a meter |
| 🟡 **B3** | Are the AXIS blocks differential or single-ended? | Ohmmeter on the unpowered board |
| 🟡 **B4** | Do the drives expect 5 V or 24 V signalling? | Drive markings / datasheet |
| 🟡 **B5** | Is the declared clock frequency real? | Scope on a step pulse |
| 🟡 **B6** | Do the isolated inputs take a 24 V sensor directly? | Optocoupler part number + meter |

**B1 is the one that can force a redesign.** The board brings out **five** inputs. A
single-motor Y needs 3 home + 1 E-stop + 1 tool sensor = exactly 5 — no margin. A
dual-motor gantry Y needs 6, which **does not fit**, and the fix depends on B2 (whether
freed encoder pins can absorb the overflow). Answer B1 before planning any wiring.

## The three findings that shape everything

If you remember nothing else from these documents, remember these.

**1. Our board is not a Mesa 7i96, and cannot be running Mesa's firmware.**
The Zhulong has six AXIS blocks, three encoder blocks and a 0–10 V analog output. Mesa's
stock `7i96d` bitfile instantiates 5 StepGens, 1 Encoder and **zero** PWMGens. A bitfile
that instantiates 5 stepgens cannot drive 6 axis connectors — so the vendor compiled
their own. Consequence: **the stock pinout is not merely shifted, it describes a
different device**, and flashing a stock Mesa bitfile onto this board is actively
dangerous. Details in [03](03-7i96-pinout.md) and [04](04-zhulong-board-hardware.md).

**2. The board's reported name string decides the HAL prefix on every line we write.**
`hm2_eth` matches board names with `strncmp(name, "7I96", 8)`, which is effectively an
exact match. A non-match is *not* fatal — the driver falls back, takes geometry from the
IDROM and works fine — but connector names become `"??"`, LED pins vanish, and the HAL
prefix follows whatever the board calls itself. Anything starting with `7I96` still
yields `hm2_7i96.0`; anything else yields something like `hm2_ZhUL.0`. **Read the prefix
off `halcmd show pin`; do not derive it by hand.** The one dangerous case is a board that
reports `7I96` but has non-3×17 geometry — that hard-aborts with a confusing message.
Decision tree in [02](02-board-bringup.md) Step 6.

**3. Step timing is scaled by a clock the driver cannot verify, and the HAL parameter
reads back as correct regardless.**
`steplen`, `stepspace`, `dirsetup` and `dirhold` are all multiplied by the IDROM's
declared `clock_frequency` to get register counts. Nothing measures the FPGA's real
clock, and the HAL parameter is only recomputed on overflow — so it returns exactly what
you typed, always. Our board carries a **50.000 MHz crystal** while the stock 7i96
declares 33 MHz. If the declared clock is wrong by 1.5×, a requested 2000 ns pulse
becomes 1320 ns on the wire with no warning anywhere, and the symptom is intermittent
lost steps that look like a mechanical fault. **Measure a pulse with a scope before
connecting a drive.** [02](02-board-bringup.md) Step 7.

## Where we are, and what comes next

**Done:** upstream reconnaissance, board identification from photographs, the bring-up
runbook, the detection script, the Pi platform runbook.

**Not done:** anything involving hardware.

### Phase 1.5 — prove the chain on the small router first

**Plan change.** Before the marble machine, we retrofit a small existing Arduino +
CNC-shield router: replace the Arduino with the Pi + Zhulong, keep the shield and its
stepper drivers.

**Why this is worth a detour:** it separates the two things that can go wrong. Debugging a
new controller chain *and* unfamiliar 2-tonne mechanics simultaneously is how projects
stall. After Phase 1.5, **the marble machine's only unknown is its own mechanics** — the
Pi, the board, the driver, the HAL structure and the step timing will all be proven.

It is also the cheap place to make mistakes. A wrong `SCALE` on the test router costs a
scrapped bit; on the marble machine it costs a gantry.

Scope limits for Phase 1.5: **no spindle control of any kind** (the test machine has
none), so nothing exercises PWM or the 0–10 V output. That work waits for the marble
machine.

Known unknowns about the test machine: firmware is *assumed* GRBL (a `$$` dump settles
it); the stepper driver type needs a photograph; and whether GRBL was ever configured to
use its physically-present end switches is unknown until `$21`/`$22` are read.

### Order of work

1. **Answer B1 — count the motors on Y** on the *marble* machine
   ([OPEN-QUESTIONS.md](OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)). One trip to the cabinet, and it decides
   whether five inputs are enough. Independent of Phase 1.5, so do it whenever convenient.
2. **Capture `$I` and `$$` from the test router**, saved raw into `board-dumps/` —
   [06](06-grbl-to-linuxcnc.md) "Obtaining the dump". Confirm it really is GRBL 1.1.
3. **Photograph the CNC shield** — the driver identity determines `STEPSPACE`, `DIRSETUP`
   and `DIRHOLD`, which GRBL has no settings for at all.
4. **Run `scripts/grbl2ini.py`** on the dump and read its warning block.
5. **Image the Pi** and validate latency — [05](05-pi-setup.md) §a, §b.
6. **Configure the network** — [05](05-pi-setup.md) §c.
7. **Power the Zhulong on the bench and run `detect-board.sh`** —
   [02](02-board-bringup.md) Steps 1–3. Bench first; do not combine an unproven board with
   an unproven config.
8. **Answer the seven questions and resolve Outcome A/B/C** —
   [02](02-board-bringup.md) §3.4 and Step 6.
9. **Verify step timing with a scope** — [02](02-board-bringup.md) Step 7. **Phase 1.5 is
   the right moment for this**: cheap machine, no consequences, and it closes blocker B5.
10. **Assemble the test router's INI and HAL** in `../configs/`, from converted fragments
    plus the hand-filled TODOs.
11. **Jog each axis a short distance** and confirm direction *before* homing. Fix a
    reversed axis by negating `SCALE`.
12. **Home, then cut something.** That is Phase 1.5 complete.
13. **Then** return to the marble machine: fill in
    [01-machine-survey.md](01-machine-survey.md) *before* its old controller is removed.

No INI or HAL exists yet. That is deliberate: the board name sets the HAL prefix and the
pin map sets every signal name, so writing configuration before steps 7–8 means writing it
twice. `grbl2ini.py` emits *fragments* for the same reason.

## Conventions used in these documents

- **Source citations** are file-and-line into `reference/`, e.g.
  [hm2_eth.c:1319](../reference/linuxcnc/src/hal/drivers/mesa-hostmot2/hm2_eth.c#L1319).
  All 58 such links were checked to resolve with in-range line numbers, and their content
  anchors verified, as of the last edit. `reference/` is gitignored — re-clone with the
  commands in [00](00-upstream-reference-map.md) if it is missing.
- **OBSERVED / INFERRED / UNVERIFIED** in [04](04-zhulong-board-hardware.md) distinguish
  photograph evidence from reasoning from guesswork. The distinction is enforced
  strictly.
- **"Not settled by source"** call-outs mark questions the LinuxCNC tree does not answer.
  These are deliberately *not* filled in with general knowledge.
- **`<B>`** in HAL names stands for the board prefix, e.g. `hm2_7i96.0`.
